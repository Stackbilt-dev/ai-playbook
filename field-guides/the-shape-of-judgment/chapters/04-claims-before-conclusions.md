# 4. Claims Before Conclusions

A conclusion can be fluent while its supporting statements disagree about time, scope, obligation, or certainty. Before asking whether the conclusion sounds right, make its claims inspectable.

## Principle

Separate logical consistency from empirical support. A claim may be internally consistent and false, well-supported and irrelevant, or probabilistically useful without being certain.

Use a small claim ledger:

| Field | Purpose |
|-------|---------|
| Claim | One statement that can be challenged independently |
| Type | Observation, inference, forecast, preference, or obligation |
| Scope | Population, resource, location, or decision it applies to |
| Observed at | When the source or system recorded the claim |
| Validity | Valid from, valid until, and any claim it supersedes |
| Relevance | Why the claim bears on this decision now |
| Provenance | Source, transformation, and responsible owner |
| Confidence | Evidence class or calibrated estimate—not prose intensity |
| Conflicts | Claims that cannot simultaneously govern the decision |
| Decision use | What transition or conclusion depends on it |

## Failure Mode: Category Collapse

Category collapse occurs when different claim types are written as if they had the same authority:

- “Conversion declined last month” is an observation.
- “The message caused the decline” is an inference.
- “Changing it will recover revenue” is a forecast.
- “Recovery matters more than consistency” is a preference.
- “The team must obtain consent” is an obligation if backed by policy or law.

Blending them into one paragraph makes the conclusion difficult to audit and easy to overstate.

Recency creates a related collapse. A recently retrieved claim may be irrelevant,
and a highly relevant claim may no longer be true. Relevance, confidence, and
temporal validity are separate properties. None should be used as a proxy for
the others.

## Mechanism: Contradiction and Constraint Review

Before synthesis, check:

1. Do two claims refer to different populations or time periods?
2. Does a forecast masquerade as an observation?
3. Does an obligation have an authoritative source?
4. Does the proposed conclusion require a claim that is absent?
5. Are confidence and evidence quality independent?
6. Can conflicting claims coexist under different scopes?
7. Has a current claim superseded rather than erased its predecessor?

Formal logic can help when propositions and rules are truly specified. Naming a logic family without defining representation or inference rules adds no protection.

## Tradeoff

Atomic claims can strip away context and produce a false sense of precision. Preserve links back to the original source and decision narrative. The ledger should make synthesis reviewable, not pretend that all judgment reduces to Boolean proof.

## Field Test

Give reviewers the conclusion without its claim ledger, then with it. Record whether the ledger changes identified gaps, confidence, or the decision route.

## Falsifier

If decomposition repeatedly creates clerical work without revealing conflicts, unsupported transitions, or scope errors, reduce the number of claims you track. Focus on statements that can change the decision.

## Authority Boundary

The system may synthesize an option from claims. An unresolved contradiction in a required claim should block authoritative transition rather than be smoothed over in prose.
